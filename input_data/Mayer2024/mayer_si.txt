S1     The Swiss Sector-Coupled Energy System
This section describes the Swiss sector-coupled energy system model. Section S1.1 discusses the
demands for each sector, Section S1.2 provides the techno-economic parameters for all conversion and
storage technologies, Section S1.3 shows the calculation of GWP100-based climate change impacts, and
Sections S1.4 and S1.5 provide more details on electricity and heat-generating technologies.

S1.1     Demands
  Sections S1.1.1, S1.1.2, and S1.1.3 describe how demands are calculated for non-industrial electricity,
residential heat, and private transportation, respectively. Regarding spatial resolution, the demands are
aggregated for all of Switzerland, which is represented as a 1-node system. Regarding temporal resolution,
the demands are resolved hourly for the year 2050. In many cases, we start with data for the year 2019 and
project it for the year 2050 based on assumptions from literature. Industrial energy demands are discussed
in Section 2.2 of the main paper.
S1.1.1   Non-industrial electricity demand
  The hourly electricity demand profile is derived from data provided by SwissGrid (Swissgrid AG, 2020),
who provide electricity consumption in 15-minute increments for the year 2019. As we consider the year
2050, we perform several adaptations to the 2019 electricity demand: First, as the Swiss population is
projected to grow from 8.69 million inhabitants in 2019 to 10.44 million inhabitants in 2050 (Federal
Statistical Office (FSO), 2020), we scale the electricity demand accordingly. Second, we assume a 20%
electricity demand reduction in 2050 due to efficiency gains (The Swiss Confederation, Fedlex - The
publication platform for the federal law, 2021; Swiss Federal Office of Energy (SFOE), 2013; Swiss Federal
Office of Energy (SFOE), Prognos AG, 2012). We exclude the 6 TWh yearly industrial electricity demand
since these are accounted for in the modelling of industrial production (Section 2.2 of the main paper).
 Additionally, we exclude the electricity demands used for residential heating applications, since these
demands are accounted for in the heating sector (Section S1.1.2). Heat demands for space heating and
warm water are provided by the Swiss Federal office of Energy (SFOE)(Swiss Federal Office of Energy

(SFOE), Prognos AG, TEP Energy GmbH, INFRAS AG, 2020). The demands are grouped by the energy
sources used to supply the demands. In 2019, 13% of the heat demand of private households and 5.5% of

To resolve double counting, we subtract the power consumption for residential heat from the electricity
demand.
  Our assumptions result in a final electricity demand of 45.2 TWh for 2050.
S1.1.2    Residential heat demand

  Our model’s residential heat demand is composed of the space heating and warm water demands of
private households and the commercial sector, provided by the Swiss Federal office of Energy (SFOE)
(Swiss Federal Office of Energy (SFOE), Prognos AG, TEP Energy GmbH, INFRAS AG, 2020). The
national residential heat demand for Switzerland in 2019 was 74.7 TWh. As we consider the year 2050
in our model, we apply a 11% reduction to the heat demand (Scenario ZERO in Swiss Federal Office
of Energy (SFOE) (2021)). This heat demand reduction accounts for climate change and the projected
population growth.
  To represent the residential heat demand in time, we use the approach of Baumgärtner et al. (2021). The
residential heat demands for the commercial sector are assumed constant throughout the year. Following
Baumgärtner et al. (2021), we model the temporal profile of the private household residential heat demand
using the hourly outdoor temperature of Switzerland (Renewables.ninja, 2021; Gelaro and et al., 2017) as
follows:

 1) Days are assumed to have a heating demand only if the average daily outside temperature is above
    12°C (Swiss Federal Office of Energy (SFOE), 2020b). Otherwise, the heat demand of the entire day is
    assumed zero.
 2) For days that have a heat demand, the hourly demand is proportional to the normalized temperature
    difference between the target indoor temperature (20°C (Swiss Federal Office of Energy (SFOE),
    2020b)) and the outdoor temperature. This temperature difference is normalized by the sum of the
    temperature difference for the entire year. Outdoor temperatures are taken from Renewables.ninja
    (2021); Gelaro and et al. (2017) for the year 2019.

S1.1.3    Private transportation demand

  For the total private transportation demand in 2050, we use the forecast from the STEM model (Panos
and Ramachandran, 2017; Prognos AG, 2012) of 67.1×109 vehicle kilometers (vkm). We derive an hourly
demand profile ftv based on traffic census data (Federal Statistical Office (FSO), 2014) that includes average
motorized vehicle counts per hour of a day at different measure points in Switzerland. We use data from
ten measure points from the last traffic census in 2013 (Figure S1).
  To derive the hourly private transportation demand profile for our model ftv , we normalize the hourly
traffic counts of each measure point by the average and take the mean of the ten measure points at each
hour. The resulting mean profile represents the ten measure points well (Figure S2).
  The hourly private transportation demand is calculated with (Eq. S1). Every day in our model follows the
same profile. Note further that any motorized vehicle is counted in the traffic census and not only private
transportation. However, we assume that our hourly profile is proportional to passenger car traffic and is
thus applicable to the private transportation demand.

S1.2        Techno-economic parameters of conversion and storage technologies


  The characteristics of all technologies included in our model are listed and described below. Details about
electricity and heat sector technologies that require additional input data are found in Sections S1.4 and
S1.5. In this section, we first present the costs and lifetimes (Section S1.2.1), including a more detailed
explanation of the direct air capture (DAC) costs. We then present the technology efficiencies and capacity
factors (Section S1.2.2).

S1.2.1           Costs and lifetimes


  In our model, all costs are given in EUR, implemented with a fixed average exchange rate between EUR
and CHF of 2019 (1:1.1125) (Federal Tax Administration (FTA), 2020). Except for direct air capture,
which has operating costs, all technologies in our model have investment and fixed, yearly maintenance
costs. The operational costs consist purely of fuel costs and are thus already counted in the import costs.


  We annualize the investment costs of technologies with an interest rate of 5% and we use the minimum
of the technology lifetime and the economic investment time of 30 years for the annualization, as described
in Baumgärtner et al. (2021). The lifetimes of all technologies are listed in Table S1, S2, S3, and S4.
Following the Swiss Energy strategy (Swiss Federal Office of Energy (SFOE), 2021), we assume 100%
electrification in private transportation in 2050. Thus, we assume that all transportation demand is satisfied
by battery electric vehicles (BEVs). As we focus on energy provision, we do not include the investment
costs of electric vehicles in our cost calculation.

S1.2.2       Efficiencies and secured capacity factors

  As explained by Baumgärtner et al. (2021), time series aggregation removes extreme load points from
the optimization. Therefore, installed capacities of generation and storage technologies are likely to be
underestimated. The secured capacity factor is introduced as a parameter to guarantee that the total available
production through generation and storage of a given product is greater than the peak product demand,
comprising both exogenous and sector-coupled endogenous demands.

S1.3        Life Cycle Inventories
  Life cycle inventories (LCIs) are used calculate the operational climate change impacts (GWP100), which
we constrain to net-zero. We use the same inventories as Baumgärtner et al. (2021) while updating the data
to ecoinvent 3.7.1 APOS (Wernet et al., 2016) (rather than 3.5 APOS) and changing the locations from
Germany to Switzerland wherever possible.
  Processes in the LCIs can contain energy-related environmental impacts from, for instance, electricity
production. However, since we explicitly model the energy sectors in our energy system, the energy-related
environmental impacts are already accounted for within our system boundary. To prevent double counting,
we delete the energy-related environmental impacts that are already accounted for in the Swiss energy
sectors. For the deletion, we follow the approach of Volkart et al. (2018) and Reinert et al. (2021). Moreover,
as our model considers the year 2050, we employ the dynamic LCA method from Reinert et al. (2021)
with the updated ecoinvent 3.7.1.

  The sections below describe the modelling details beyond techno-economic parameters of electricity

can be found in Section S1.2. Unless otherwise specified, the data for electricity generation technologies is
taken from the open-source platform Open Power Systems Data (OPSD), which is divided into data for
conventional (Open Power System Data, 2020a) and renewable (Open Power System Data, 2020b) power
plants. Additionally, unless otherwise stated, techno-economic parameters are taken from Baumgärtner et
al. (2021).

S1.4.1    Hydropower




  Hydropower includes electricity production and storage technologies and well as combinations of the
two. In our model, we implement four different hydropower technologies: run-of-river, pure large dam,
pure pumped storage hydro (PSH), and a hybrid hydropower plant consisting of a large dam with PSH.
Run-of-river power plants use the energy from flowing water to produce electricity. Large dams accumulate
flowing water in a reservoir, subsequently releasing it for electricity production. PSH stores energy by
pumping water into a reservoir at a higher elevation, producing electricity from the previously pumped
water. Hybrid hydropower plants consist of both a PSH and a large dam, producing electricity from both
natural inflow and previously pumped water (Figure S3).



  To model the hybrid hydropower plant, we divide the hybrid large dam and hybrid PSH into two separate
technologies and couple them via the power production capacity, P h [MW], equating it to both the power
production capacity for hybrid large dam, P ld,h , and to the power production/storage capacity for the
hybrid PSH, P psh,h (Equation S2). The power production/storage capacity for the hybrid PSH is calculated
by multiplying the hybrid PSH energy storage capacity, E psh,h [MWh], by its flow-to-storage capacity
factor, f psh . To calculate the flow-to-storage capacity factor, we divide the total existing Swiss PSH turbine
capacity, P psh,tc (3.52 GW (Open Power System Data, 2020a)), by 90% of the total existing storage
volume of Swiss PSH reservoirs, E psh,sc (8.8 TWh (Felix et al., 2020)) (Equation S3). The 90% is assumed
due to the reported 10% of unused reservoir volume that is always kept as backup (Swiss Federal Office of
Energy (SFOE), 2020a; Felix et al., 2020).



  To limit the simultaneous production of the hybrid large dam, Ptld,h , and the hybrid PSH system, Ptpsh,h ,
at a given time step, t, we add a constraint that limits the total simultaneous production from the hybrid
system to its existing power production capacity (Equation S4).

Our maximum potential PV capacity for the year 2050 is 67 TWh (Federal Department of the Environment,

S1.4.3    Biogas and Geothermal
  Our biogas-to-power electricity capacity includes all biomass and biogas power plants, sewage and landfill
gas power plants, and other bioenergy and renewable waste plants from the OPSD data for Switzerland,
yielding a total generation capacity of 87.2 MW (Open Power System Data, 2020b). All biogas technologies
are represented by a single technology with a constant hourly availability of 57% (Kannan and Turton,
2011), or 50 MWh. We justify this simplified implementation since bioenergy technologies are similar and
have a low overall contribution to the Swiss electricity mix now and in future decades (Swiss Federal Office
of Energy (SFOE), 2013). Biogas-to-power plants are modeled as a pure power-producing technology,
without heat cogeneration.
  Regarding geothermal electricity, we set the potential capacity in 2050 to 2 TWh (Swiss Federal Office
of Energy (SFOE), 2021) and assume a constant hourly availability.

S1.5     Heating technologies
S1.5.1    Heat pumps and electrode boilers
  Heat pumps, electrode boilers, and gas burners are used to supply heat at different temperature levels
(Table 2). However, heat pumps are not an option for the entire low temperature (LT) range in our model.
Similarly, electrode boilers are not an option over the entire medium temperature (MT) range. Although
heat pumps with a heat supply for temperatures above 100°C are already under development, they are not
commercially available (Arpagaus et al., 2017). Similar holds true for electrode boilers, for which different
sources define a maximum temperature level between 350°C-500°C (Antoni et al., 2015; Forschungsstelle
für Energiewirtschaft e.V. (FfE), 2017; PARAT Halvorsen AS, 2021). Therefore, we limit the potential
capacities of LT heat pumps and MT electrode boilers in our model. The LT heat pump’s capacities are
constrained to supply at maximum the industrial process heat demand between 0-100°C, and the MT
electrode boiler’s capacities are constrained to the demand between 200°C-400°C. However, LT heat pumps
in our model are free to supply additional heat for DAC.
S1.5.2    Energetic rehabilitation
  We constrain energetic rehabilitation such that it can at maximum provide 50% of the civil heat demand.
This implementation is in accordance with a study by Palzer on Germany (Palzer, 2016), who show that
the rehabilitation increases the thermal insulation and results in a 50% reduction of the heating demand of
a building. The Swiss advisory platform Energieheld (Energieheld Schweiz, 2021) also refers to German
data (co2online, 2021) when defining the reduction potential for Swiss buildings to be around 50%.
